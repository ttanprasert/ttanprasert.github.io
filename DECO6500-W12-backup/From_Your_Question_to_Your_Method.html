Week 11 · Assessment 3 · from question to method

From Your Question to Your Method

A worked example and practice for getting from your A3 question to a method that collects evidence about the future. Work through it in Friday’s applied class, and come back to it whenever you revise Section 4.

Tuesday’s loop, with one arrow opened up. On Tuesday the loop went: your question → what evidence would answer it → what method helps you collect it. Here we open up the first arrow. You cannot say what evidence your question needs until you know what makes the change happen at the point your question is about. That is the mechanism. The mechanism points to a lens. The lens tells you what evidence to collect. The futuring technique decides how you collect it from one step into the future, not only about today.

The chain: six steps from your question to a claim you can defend

  1. Step 1 Leverage point. Where in the system does your intervention push, or where does its effect land? Your question is about this point. Why it matters: it narrows the whole system to the place your question is about.
    Go to: Worked example
  2. Step 2 Mechanism. At that point, what actually makes the change happen or fail? Mainly people’s behaviour, how well a process or organisation works, or social factors (routines, relationships, norms). Why it matters: different mechanisms need different evidence. If several apply, choose the one your question depends on most, and say why.
    Go to: Mechanism to lens · Worked example
  3. Step 3 Lens. Which theory explains that mechanism? Start from the lenses on the Week 10 method guide page (see the table below). Why it matters: the lens gives you the parts to look at, for example capability, opportunity and motivation.
    Go to: Mechanism to lens · The eight lenses · Worked example
  4. Step 4 Evidence. What would show you each part of the lens? Which kinds of evidence can capture it, and what is the gap? Why it matters: the lens decides what you collect. Without it, you collect what is easy to find.
    Go to: Kinds of evidence · Worked example
  5. Step 5 Futuring adaptation. How will you collect that evidence from one step into the future, and not only about today? Why it matters: this is what makes it an A3 method and not an audit of today.
    Go to: Four ways · Two-deletion test · Futuring techniques · Worked example
  6. Step 6 Grounded future claim. What could you say at the end, and what would it rest on? Why it matters: this leads to your strongest-claim statement and your criteria (Section 4).
    Go to: Claim frame · Labels · Strongest claim and criteria

Step 2 to Step 3: from mechanism to lens

If the key mechanism is…Start from this lensMethod guide lens
People’s behaviourCOM-B or the Theoretical Domains Framework; Diffusion of Innovations or RE-AIM if the behaviour is taking it up and keeping itLens 3; Lens 5
How well a process or organisation worksNormalisation Process Theory; NASSS if the process depends on technology or infrastructure workingLens 2; Lens 1
Social factors: routines, relationships, norms, powerSocial practice theory; Value Sensitive Design or power–interest mappingLens 6; Lens 7

Rules and funding (Lens 4) and harm and exclusion (Lens 8) usually work as a second lens: they show what limits the mechanism, or what could go wrong when it works. This is the same idea as the method guide’s “If your intervention mainly…” list, now starting from the mechanism.

Step 5: four ways to collect evidence from one step into the future

No evidence comes from the future. So your method has to collect evidence now, in a way that reaches forward. There are four ways to do this. Most good methods combine two.

WayWhat you doWhat grounds itThe gap to name
1 Stand people in the futureMethod guide: timeline at time points; pre-mortem in the futureGive people who hold the role a short scenario set at Year 1 or Year 3. Ask what they would do, and why. Build the scenario from your evidence.The people hold the role. The scenario rests on documents and cases.What people expect, not what they did.
2 Find where the future already existsMethod guide: timeline at time points; backcastingStudy a place, a group or an earlier time where something like your intervention has already happened.Real outcomes that already happened.It is never quite your system. Argue what transfers.
3 Measure now, then move it forwardMethod guide: Futures Wheel (evidence on every arrow); Three Horizons; futures coneMeasure the parts of your lens as they are today. Then use the futuring technique to reason, step by step, how the intervention shifts them.Every step starts from something you measured.The forward steps are your reasoning. Label them as inferred.
4 Probe with something from the futureDesign fiction (Week 10 lecture, scenario family; not on the method guide)Make an artefact from Year 3 (a mock screen, a letter, a news story, a policy) and record how people react to it.The reactions are real, and collected now.You made the artefact, so your design shapes the reactions.

The lens decides what you look at. The futuring technique decides from when, or where, you look.

If your scenario does not ask about the parts of your lens, the two are not connected yet. If the parts of your lens are only asked about today, you have not reached the future yet.

Check your method: the two-deletion test

Delete…Then ask
The futuring techniqueWould the evidence you collect be any different? If not, the futuring is decoration and you have an audit of today. If it would, the futuring is doing real work.
The evidenceDoes your future still stand? If it does, it is speculation. Every time point needs something you collected or read underneath it.

Step 6: the grounded future claim

Because [what your evidence shows now, or elsewhere] and [what your intervention changes, through the mechanism], I anticipate [what happens, at which time point, for whom], provided [the conditions it depends on]. I would be wrong if [what you would expect to see if the claim were false].

A claim with conditions can be defended. A prediction with no conditions is speculation. Label each part of a claim so a reader can see what it rests on:

LabelWhat it means
DocumentedYou can point to it: a source you read, or something you collected, including what people told you they expect.
InferredYour reading of documented evidence, and your reasoning from it, using your lens. The brief calls these interpretation and inference.
AnticipatedA plausible anticipation: what could reasonably happen at a future time point, with what it rests on shown.
SpeculationNothing underneath it yet. Find evidence, say it is speculation, or drop it.

What “plausible anticipation” means

The brief asks you to tell evidence apart from interpretation, inference and plausible anticipation. In plain words, a plausible anticipation is a future that could reasonably happen, given your evidence. It does not have to be certain, or even the most likely; it has to be believable, and you have to be able to show what makes it believable. On the method guide’s futures cone, it sits in the plausible band. A future with nothing underneath it is speculation.

Using AI?

You can use it, for example to draft a scenario or an artefact. Every line still needs evidence underneath it, and be ready to say why you used AI, how, and what you checked.

Part A · Worked example: who puts it right? Whole room

The case (Week 9)

It is 2027. A new Australian law means engagement features (endless scroll, autoplay, recommendation feeds) must not operate for anyone under 16. To comply, a major platform now checks the age of every Australian user. For anyone assessed as under 16, the feed stops. The case is fictional and is modelled on a proposal that is not law.

This example is illustrative. The sources named are the kinds you would look for; check any detail before you rely on it. It shows the thinking, not a finished Section 4.

Question (from Tuesday): When the age check gets it wrong, who puts it right, and what does that do to schools?

1 Leverage point

The point where a wrong age decision gets corrected. The platform offers an appeal, but a student who is wrongly assessed as under 16 often needs an adult’s help to make one: a parent, or the school staff they already go to with online problems. So the leverage point is the correction pathway, and specifically its school end.

Where did this come from? In the Week 9 Tomorrow Party, several zones saw this work drift to school wellbeing staff. That record is not evidence. It is a lead: it told us where to look.

2 Mechanism

CandidateWhat it would mean hereDecision
BehaviourWhether students ask for help, or give up and find a way around the check.Matters, but the question asks who fixes it, not what students do.
Social factorsWhether students come to trust some adults more, or less.Relevant, but it follows from the main change.
How well a process worksWhether helping students correct wrong age decisions becomes workable, routine work in schools, or is absorbed by whoever is nearest, with no time or resources.Chosen. “What does that do to schools?” depends on whether a new task gets done, and kept up.

What this choice leaves out: students’ own behaviour and their voice. That becomes a named gap (Perspectives).

3 Lens

Normalisation Process Theory (Lens 2 on the method guide). It explains how a new way of working becomes a normal part of everyday work, or does not. Its four parts become the things to look at: coherence (do staff see this as their work?), cognitive participation (who commits to it?), collective action (can the work actually be done?) and reflexive monitoring (does anyone record or review it?).

Why not NASSS? NASSS would suit a question about whether the age-check technology works. Here the technology is given. The question is about new work inside an organisation.

Why not harms modelling, as on Tuesday? Tuesday’s lecture used harms modelling (Lens 8) on this question to show the loop. Starting from the mechanism shows that the question depends most on whether new work becomes routine, so NPT is the main lens here; harm still works well as a second lens.

5 Futuring adaptation

Two of the four ways, combined. Way 2 (find where the future already exists): the first months of the December 2025 rule are the closest thing to Year 1 that has already happened. Way 1 (stand people in the future): a Year 1 and a Year 3 scenario for wellbeing staff. Following the method guide’s advice for Lens 2, the Year 1 scenario asks about coherence and commitment; the Year 3 scenario asks about collective action and monitoring.

Every line of the scenario comes from Step 4. What the appeal asks for comes from the help pages. Which students are misjudged comes from the trial report. That is what stops the scenario being a story.

Year 1 scenario (extract). It is March 2028. A Year 11 student comes to you. The platform has assessed her as under 16 and her feed has stopped. To appeal she must upload ID, or have a parent confirm her age. Her parent works away. What do you do? Who else would you involve? Is this part of your job?

Year 3 scenario (extract). It is 2030. Cases like this now come to you regularly. What has changed in how your service runs? What has stopped to make room? Does anyone record these cases, or ask about them?

One detail has no anchor yet: how often cases come in. Rather than invent a number, run the Year 3 scenario in two versions (occasional and every week) and compare the answers. If you cannot ground a detail, make it a variable.

The method in one sentence: “I adapted Normalisation Process Theory by turning its four parts into a Year 1 and a Year 3 scenario, built from the platform’s appeal steps and the age-assurance trial findings, which I put to three school wellbeing staff and compared with how schools responded to the December 2025 rule, because my question is whether helping students correct wrong age decisions becomes routine school work or is quietly absorbed by whoever is nearest.”

Two-deletion testResult
Delete the scenariosThe evidence changes: I would collect only today’s policy and what happened in 2025, an audit plus history with nothing about Year 3. The futuring is doing real work.
Delete the evidenceThe scenarios become my story about schools, and staff would be reacting to something I invented. The evidence is doing real work.

6 A grounded future claim this method could produce

This is the shape of a claim, not a finding. The real claim depends on what the evidence shows.

Because school policy makes wellbeing staff the first contact for students’ online-safety problems [documented], and because an appeal asks for ID or a parent’s confirmation that some students cannot easily get [documented], I anticipate that by Year 1 wellbeing staff will be helping students through appeals without it being anyone’s assigned task [anticipated: rests on what staff say in the Year 1 scenario], provided the platform does not resolve most appeals on its own within days. By Year 3, this unassigned work is likely to go unrecorded [inferred, from the monitoring part of NPT and the policy’s silence]. I would be wrong if staff say they would refer these cases elsewhere, or if schools did not take on this work after the 2025 rule.

Into Section 4: strongest claim and criteria

Strongest claim. If everything goes to plan, this method can show whether the staff who would carry this work see it as theirs and expect to keep it up at Year 3, and which part of NPT is most likely to fail first, grounded in current policy and one comparable case. It cannot show how many students will need help, what students themselves experience, or what actually happens in schools in 2030.

CriterionMy method works well on this if…, which I will know because…
Groundingevery line of the Year 1 and Year 3 scenarios traces to a named document or to the 2025 case, which I will know because my scenario table gives a source for each line.
Perspectivesit includes at least two roles who would do the work, not only a principal, which I will know from who I interviewed. Students are named as missing.
Answerabilitythe interviews give answers on all four parts of NPT at both time points, which I will know by coding the notes against the four parts and marking any gaps.
Part B · Your turn, partly done: who is shut out? Pairs

In pairs. Steps 1 to 3 are done for you. Read them, then complete Steps 4 to 6. Use your Tuesday brainstorm (worksheet page 1): it was on this question.

Question (from Tuesday’s brainstorm): Which students does the age check shut out, and what happens to them by Year 3?

1 Leverage point (done)

The moment a young person is assessed as under 16, rightly or wrongly, and their feed stops. The question is about what they do next. Note that “shut out” covers two groups: under-16s who are correctly assessed, and older students who are wrongly assessed. Choose one, or compare them.

2 Mechanism (done)

Behaviour. What happens to them by Year 3 depends mainly on what they do: appeal, accept the plain feed, borrow an account, or move to smaller apps and group chats. Considered and set aside: how well a process works (the appeals pathway; that was Part A) and social factors (who they turn to; that is Part C).

3 Lens (done)

COM-B (Lens 3 on the method guide). A behaviour happens only when a person has the capability (knowledge, skills), the opportunity (time, resources, what people around them do) and the motivation (plans, habits, emotions). For example, a student can only borrow an account if they know how, have someone to borrow from, and want the feed back enough to bother.
